Bank Customer Churn

20.4% churn — and €186M of deposits sitting behind it.

Scale
10,000 customers · France, Germany, Spain
Role
Sole analyst — raw extract to dashboard
Source
[[REPLACE: dataset name, link and licence]]

Context

A retail bank holding a €765M deposit book across France, Germany and Spain needs to know how much of that book is at risk of walking out of the door, and which customers a retention budget should reach first.

Churn is normally reported as a customer count, which is the wrong unit for this decision. A bank does not lose customers, it loses deposits — and the customers most likely to leave are not evenly distributed across balance sizes. So the question is not “what is our churn rate”, it is “how much money is behind it, and where is that money concentrated”.

The data

[[REPLACE: dataset name and link]] — 10,000 retail-banking customers across three markets. One row per customer, carrying balance, tenure, product count, an activity flag, and a churn indicator.

One property of the data shapes everything downstream: at 20.4% churn the majority class is 79.6%, so any rule or model has to beat a do-nothing baseline of 79.6% accuracy before it is worth anything at all. That is the number to quote whenever somebody reports an 80%-accurate churn model.

Approach

I built the analysis in the unit the business actually manages — euros of deposit — rather than in customer counts, then cut it by the dimensions a retention team can act on: geography, product holding, balance band and activity.

Two cuts carried the result.

Germany churns at 32.4% against 16.2% in France and 16.7% in Spain — roughly double the rest on a comparable customer base. That is not a customer-behaviour finding, it is an operational or competitive one, and it points at a specific market rather than at a generic retention campaign.

Product holding runs the other way, and much harder. Churn is 28% at one product, falls to 8% at two, then climbs to 83% at three and 100% at four. If the relationship were monotonic the reading would be the familiar “more products, more sticky”. It is not monotonic, so two products is the healthy state and anything above it is a signal of something else. [[REPLACE: your read on why three- and four-product holders churn almost universally — bundled or pushed sales, a distressed-customer pattern, or a small-sample effect. Quote the group sizes (n) for the three- and four-product segments so a reader can judge how much weight the 83% and 100% carry.]]

Why segmentation rather than a churn model. A logistic regression would return a churn probability per customer, and it would have been quick to fit. But the decision on the table is where to point a retention budget, not which individual to telephone first. A model would have added a layer needing its own explanation without changing the answer that segmentation already gives — and it would have had to clear that 79.6% baseline to earn its place.

Result

20.4% churn, €186M of deposits at risk — 24% of a €765M book — and roughly €4.6M of annual revenue behind it.

The concentration is what makes it actionable. Customers holding more than €100,000 — 4,799 of them — churn at 25.2%, against the 20.4% average. The deposit book is therefore leaking faster than the customer count implies, and a retention effort aimed at Germany and at three-and-four-product holders reaches a disproportionate share of the money rather than a disproportionate share of the people.

What this cannot show. The €4.6M revenue-at-risk figure applies an illustrative ~2.5% net interest margin — it indicates scale, it is not the bank’s actual margin. And this is a cross-sectional snapshot: it records which customers have churned, not when or in what order, so it cannot support a time-to-churn or survival claim without a dated event history.
