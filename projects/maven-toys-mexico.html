Case study · Margin

Maven Toys México

The entire margin decline was product mix. The rate effect was zero.

Scale
829,262 transaction lines · 50 stores · 29 cities
Role
Sole analyst — raw extract to dashboard

Context

A 50-store toy retailer across 29 Mexican cities grew revenue 30.9% year on year. Gross profit only grew 16.0%.

That gap needs explaining before the next buying cycle, because the answer decides who acts on it. If products are selling at worse margins, it’s a pricing problem. If the shops are just selling more of the cheap stuff, it’s an assortment problem. Different teams, different budgets.

The data

A public dataset of 829,262 transaction lines from 50 stores across 29 cities, joined to a product table with cost and retail price, and a store table with location and opening date.

Cost comes from the product record, not the transaction line. So gross profit is calculated at current cost rather than cost at the time of sale, which means any mid-period cost change gets applied backwards across the whole history. Worth stating up front, because it bounds what the rest of the analysis can claim.

Approach

Revenue up 30.9%, gross profit up 16.0%, gross margin down 3.35 percentage points. A margin move has two possible sources: you sold a different mix of things (mix), or you sold the same things at different margins (rate). The job is to split the decline between the two instead of arguing about it.

I ran an exact mix-vs-rate bridge at category level. It put 100% of the −3.35pp decline on mix, with a rate effect of exactly zero. Category margins didn’t move at all. What changed was how much of each category was in the basket.

Art & Crafts grew $1.29M year on year while Electronics fell $311K: a swing of more than $1.6M between two categories that earn very different margins.

Why an exact bridge and not just reading the chart. The category chart shows the mix shifted. It doesn’t show how much of the margin decline that accounts for, and “mostly mix” and “100% mix, 0% rate” are two different conversations with a buying team. The bridge also has to sum to the observed −3.35pp, so if I’d got it wrong the arithmetic would have said so.

Result

All 3.35 points of margin decline attributed to mix. Rate effect: zero.

The chain bought its growth with margin, and the bridge says how much it paid. The fix belongs with assortment and promotional planning, not pricing — and pricing is where “our margins are falling” usually sends a team working on instinct. A price response would have been aimed at a rate effect that isn’t there.

The monthly series shows the same thing over time: steady erosion from 30.8% in January 2022 to a low of 25.3% in July 2023, then a recovery to 27.4% by September. I can’t tell from this data whether that recovery was deliberate or the mix drifting back on its own, and it’s the first thing I’d want to ask about.

On this evidence I’d take it to the buying team as a category-weighting question: put a margin-mix target next to the revenue target, so growth in a low-margin category is planned for rather than discovered at the end of the year.

What this can’t show. A mix-vs-rate bridge is an accounting identity, not a causal claim. It says where the margin went, not why the mix shifted. Whether Art & Crafts grew on demand, on promotion, on an assortment decision or on new store openings is a separate question this can’t answer.
